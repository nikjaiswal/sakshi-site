साक्षी Structured Accountability through Knowledge, Scrutiny, and Health Integrity

What health AI companies claim, checked against what the evidence shows.

SAKSHI is a community-operated investigative platform that documents what health AI companies claim versus what independent evidence supports, in the settings where their systems are deployed. A project of MIT Critical Data.

Status. SAKSHI is a proposal under development. No profiles have been published, and the framework’s claims have not yet been tested.

01 / What is SAKSHI

MIT Critical Data

A witness for clinical AI, where it is deployed.

In Sanskrit, sākṣī means witness: the one who is present with eyes open and sees things as they are. SAKSHI is built to place a witness where clinical AI claims currently go unverified.

It rests on three elements: trained local auditors in the settings where health AI is deployed, a standardized four-domain investigative protocol, and a public evidence record that procurement officers, health ministries, journalists, patient advocates, and legal actors can draw on.

SAKSHI cannot compel a company to change its practices. What it produces is a permanent public record of which claims held up under independent scrutiny and which did not.

01Adversarial by design

Findings are reached whether or not the company engages, from public evidence, filings, and interviews. Silence is recorded as a finding.

02Community-operated, not community-consulted

Local auditors apply the protocol, make the evidentiary judgments, and author the findings. Local knowledge sets the agenda.

03Standardized for cross-context comparison

The protocol is the same at every regional hub, so a finding from one city can be read next to one from another.

04Continuous, not periodic

Profiles are updated as the deployed system changes. The record moves with the live clinical intervention it holds to account.

The gap

Three accountability mechanisms exist. Each fails in a different place.

  • Regulatory clearance is a single gate.

    It provides real accountability at market entry, then says nothing about a system after it is granted, and it stops at national borders.

  • Technical auditing depends on access evaluators never get.

    It examines a model at a fixed moment, assumes access to internals that independent evaluators rarely obtain, and says little about how far public claims have run ahead of the evidence.

  • Tort liability presupposes legal infrastructure most deployment settings lack.

    Where liability infrastructure is thin and suing a health AI vendor has never been tested in court, a missed diagnosis from a miscalibrated algorithm leaves no trace.

02 / The Protocol

Four domains, one profile.

Every profile applies the same four-domain protocol. Together the domains cover the evidence behind a system’s claims, how it entered the market, the incentives that shape how it behaves, and its effect on the patients who encounter it.

01

Clinical evidence audit

Published claims against the actual evidence base.

  • Are there registered trials?
  • Is validation peer-reviewed?
  • Were study populations representative of the deployment context?
02

Regulatory pathway analysis

How the product entered the market and what oversight it is subject to.

  • Sandbox exemption or clearance?
  • Does clearance from one jurisdiction imply validation in another?
  • Is post-market surveillance in place?
03

Financial disclosure map

Investor and board relationships; incentive structures.

  • Do financial arrangements create misaligned incentives?
  • Does government entanglement suppress independent scrutiny?
04

Patient experience reporting

Informed consent, workflow integration, recourse mechanisms.

  • Do patients know AI is involved in their care?
  • What happens when the clinician disagrees with the output?
  • How can a patient seek recourse?

No single score. Each domain states what evidence exists, what is missing, and what the gaps mean, with the company’s reply and every correction on the record. A technical layer looks at calibration, subgroup fairness, robustness, and dataset shift alongside.

01 / SAKSHI paper

SAKSHI: A Community-Operated Investigative Platform for Accountability in Clinical AI Deployment

Al-Louzi et al. · COLM 2026, DAIH workshop · 2026

Proposes trained local auditors, a standardized four-domain investigative protocol, and a public evidence record to close the post-deployment accountability gap in clinical AI, beginning in low- and middle-income countries.

02 / Related proposal

What Utah’s clinical AI sandbox reveals about independent oversight

Gorijavolu et al. · Nature Medicine · 2026

Prompted by the same state regulatory sandbox, this piece calls for a registry of independent AI auditors in which vetted external teams stress-test systems inside secure data rooms. SAKSHI departs from it on governance: communities in deployment settings are the investigators.

Auditors

Community auditors are not listed by name. Profiles are published under the consortium’s institutional name, and individual attribution is kept in the certification record, so that legal exposure is carried collectively rather than by individual investigators. See how legal risk is distributed.
