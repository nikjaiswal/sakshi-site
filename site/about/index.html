About

About SAKSHI

A proposal for a community-operated investigative platform aimed at the post-deployment accountability gap in clinical AI.

Relationship to MIT Critical Data

SAKSHI is a project of MIT Critical Data, the Laboratory for Computational Physiology at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. The framework was developed by a multidisciplinary team of clinicians and clinical trainees, public-health and informatics researchers, and data scientists, several of them working in the deployment regions the platform targets.

The consortium's wider institutional partnerships are disclosed publicly and managed through a firewall that keeps commercial relationships from shaping which audits are chosen, how they are run, or what they conclude. An independent editorial board drawn from outside the consortium oversees that firewall. See Governance.

Where things stand

SAKSHI is a proposal under development. The paper describing it presents a design and a governance model, not an evaluation.

  • No profiles have been published.
  • The case study is a hypothetical composite.
  • None of the framework's comparability or feasibility claims have been tested against real profiles produced by independently constituted teams.
  • The Delphi-based process for setting shared evaluation thresholds is work in progress, not a finished mechanism. Until it is validated, the certification protocol reduces cross-hub inconsistency in auditor judgment without fully controlling it.
  • The first regional hubs are being formed. See Get Involved.

What SAKSHI cannot do

SAKSHI cannot force a company to change its practices, pull a product, or compensate patients who were harmed. That is a real limit.

What it produces instead is a permanent public evidentiary record that puts the absence of accountability on the record, where the actors who can do something about it will find it. Those actors vary from case to case: journalists, health ministries and procurement officials, legal advocates, patient organizations, and regulators. No one of these channels does the job alone, and SAKSHI cannot dictate which of them will act in a given case. What it can do is supply the evidence each of them needs to begin.

Some companies will absorb the reputational cost of a damaging profile and carry on unchanged. In that case the profile at least becomes a documented baseline for measuring what they do next.

Open empirical questions

The central open questions about SAKSHI are empirical, and the paper does not resolve them. It sets out how they will be tested once the first hubs are operating. Three claims carry the weight of the proposal, and each has a corresponding test planned.

Does the protocol surface findings a company or regulator would not already have?
The planned test is a comparison, on a small initial set of real deployments, between what a SAKSHI profile documents and what is independently discoverable through the regulatory filings and public disclosures already available to a company or a ministry of health. A protocol that only reorganizes information already on the record would still have value as a public, accessible record. The stronger claim, that community-operated investigation reaches evidence institutional actors do not surface on their own, requires showing profiles that turn up gaps a company's own disclosures did not: missing local validation, undisclosed financial entanglement, undocumented patient consent practices.
Do differently composed local auditor teams converge on comparable judgments?
This is a reliability question, and it will be tested the way inter-rater reliability is tested elsewhere: multiple independently constituted auditor teams applying the protocol to the same deployment, with agreement measured domain by domain rather than as a single composite score, since the domains are not equally amenable to convergence. The Delphi process is the mechanism for building the shared thresholds this test depends on. The test itself is what will show whether that process succeeded.
Does the training pipeline produce auditors capable of the evidentiary discipline the protocol assumes?
Certification currently ends in a supervised field contribution assessed by a senior auditor. The planned validation adds an external check: profiles produced by newly certified auditors are reviewed against the same reference-case rubric used in training, by evaluators not involved in that auditor's certification, to see whether performance on the training case predicts performance on a live one.

None of these tests can run before hubs exist and profiles are produced. That is why they are set out as a plan rather than reported as results. The next stage of the work is standing up the first hubs specifically to generate the data these three tests require.

This website

The source of this site is kept in a public GitHub repository. That is a transparency feature: the repository's edit history is part of the evidentiary record. Profile pages are rendered from structured data files, one per profile, so every future correction to a published finding is a visible commit rather than a silent edit.

The site is static, carries no analytics or tracking, and uses no third-party scripts. Content is licensed CC BY 4.0.
