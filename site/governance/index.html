Governance

Governance and independence

The credibility of SAKSHI findings rests on the integrity of the process that produces them.

Four design principles

Each principle responds to a specific failure mode in existing accountability approaches.

  • Adversarial by design

    SAKSHI follows the model of investigative journalism rather than peer review or certification. It reaches findings whether or not the company engages, working from public evidence, regulatory filings, and interviews. When a company does not respond, that silence is recorded as a finding.

  • Community-operated, not community-consulted

    Local auditors are investigators, not an advisory panel. They apply the protocol, make the evidentiary judgments, and author the findings. Local knowledge sets the agenda: which questions get asked, which gaps get named, and what counts as adequate evidence in a given clinical and cultural context.

  • Standardized for cross-context comparison

    The protocol is the same at every regional hub. The auditor team supplies the local context; the methodology does not change. A finding from one city can then be read next to one from another, which is what makes cross-regional patterns visible.

  • Continuous, not periodic

    Profiles are updated as the deployed system changes. What is being held to account is a live clinical intervention that keeps moving, so the record has to move with it.

Auditor training and certification

Certification is a prerequisite for working on a profile. It runs in three phases.

  1. Self-directed online module

    Covers the four investigative domains, evidence appraisal, research ethics, responsible disclosure, and the legal limits of evidence-based public reporting.

  2. Live workshop

    Trainees apply the protocol to a standardized reference case and receive structured feedback on it.

  3. Supervised field contribution

    Before being certified to work on their own, trainees contribute to a live profile under a senior auditor.

Certification lapses on a fixed cycle and is renewed by reassessing competency against an updated reference case, so that it tracks changes in the health AI field. Hub coordinators keep the hubs consistent with one another by calibrating periodically against profiles produced at other sites.

A Delphi process among hub coordinators and senior auditors is the planned mechanism for setting the shared thresholds that calibration is checked against: what counts as adequate validation evidence, and what counts as a disqualifying conflict. That process is still under way, and its output will be validated before it is treated as settled. See the evaluation plan.

The certification pipeline borrows one structural idea from enterprise information-security attestation frameworks such as SOC 2: a fixed set of control criteria, evaluated against documented evidence, by a party distinct from the one being evaluated. It does not adopt SOC 2 wholesale. SOC 2 audits are commissioned and paid for by the entity being audited, which is the exact dependency SAKSHI exists to avoid.

Conflict of interest disclosure

Before participating in any profile, auditors complete a disclosure covering three kinds of relationship:

  • financial relationships with the company under review or its direct competitors;
  • advisory or employment relationships with health ministries or procurement bodies involved in the deployment;
  • membership in civil society organizations receiving funding from health AI companies in the same therapeutic or geographic space.

The requirements follow the disclosure model used in academic peer review and equity research. Auditors with material conflicts are recused. Relationships below the recusal threshold are noted in the published profile.

Disclosure alone does not remove bias, and SAKSHI does not treat it as though it does. A disclosed conflict is a fact for the independent editorial board and, ultimately, the reader to weigh. The value of disclosure is that it moves the judgment about whether a relationship compromises a finding out of the auditor's own hands and into the editorial board's and the public record's, where it can be checked against the finding itself.

Independence from commercial interests

The platform prohibits commercial sponsorship of individual profiles and bars any entity with a financial relationship to a profiled company from participating in that profile's production or review.

This line is drawn at the institutional level, not only the individual level, because the risk that motivates it is structural rather than personal. A hub whose host institution accepts funding from a health AI company, or from an investor with a stake in one, faces pressure on its findings about that company or its competitors regardless of whether any individual auditor is personally biased. Disclosure catches the individual case; the firewall is aimed at the institutional one.

MIT Critical Data's wider institutional partnerships are disclosed publicly and managed through this firewall, which keeps commercial relationships from shaping which audits are chosen, how they are run, or what they conclude. An independent editorial board drawn from outside the consortium oversees the firewall. Its composition and conflict of interest disclosures are published alongside each profile.

Appeals and correction

When a profile is published, the company is notified and given a set window to respond. Its response can include:

  • evidence the auditors did not have;
  • a request to reassess particular findings;
  • factual corrections.

Verified corrections are appended to the profile with a timestamp and a note on what changed. The original finding is not silently rewritten. The company's reply is published with the profile, whether it accepts the findings, disputes them, or adds evidence.

This adversarial process, with a documented outcome on the record, is what keeps SAKSHI findings defensible in most jurisdictions and separates the platform from one-sided reporting. It also imposes a response burden that smaller civil-society partners may struggle to meet, a limit the project states plainly.

Companies that are the subject of a profile should write to lcp-sakshi@mit.edu.

Distributing legal risk

Scrutinizing powerful companies creates professional and legal exposure, especially for junior researchers. SAKSHI is designed to spread that risk across a global community rather than let it settle on individual investigators, through three mechanisms.

  • Profiles are published and legally accountable under the consortium's institutional name. Individual auditor attribution is kept in the certification record, not on the public document.
  • Legal counsel is retained centrally by the consortium and made available to any hub facing a legal challenge.
  • The appeals process requires companies to respond on the record rather than through informal pressure on individual auditors, which converts disputes into a documented exchange the consortium's counsel can manage.
