SAKSHI

Sākṣī, the one who sees. A project built to observe clearly and report faithfully.

SAKSHI (Structured Accountability through Knowledge, Scrutiny, and Health Integrity) is a community-operated investigative platform that documents what health AI companies claim versus what independent evidence supports. It is a project of MIT Critical Data, the Laboratory for Computational Physiology at MIT.

The name

Sākṣī means witness.

In Sanskrit, sākṣī is the observer: the one who is present with eyes open and sees things as they are. The word is formed from sa, meaning with, and akṣa, meaning eye. In Vedānta it names the awareness that watches experience without being pulled into it.

SAKSHI is built to place a witness where clinical AI claims currently go unverified. The witness neither prosecutes nor defends. It records what companies claim and what the evidence shows, and it makes that record public.

The problem

Three accountability mechanisms exist. Each fails in a different place.

Autonomous clinical AI systems are entering health markets worldwide, including places with no meaningful regulatory infrastructure, and they are arriving faster than oversight was built to track them. The current approaches each check a system once, at a single point of entry, instead of following it as it operates.

  • Regulatory clearance is a single gate.

    Regulatory frameworks provide real accountability at market entry. They do not monitor continuously, and they stop at national borders. A clearance obtained on one patient population still appears in marketing materials in every deployment context, including places where that validation says nothing about local clinical reality. Clearance says nothing about a system after it is granted.

  • Technical auditing depends on access evaluators never get.

    Red-teaming, bias evaluations, model cards and impact assessments examine model behavior at a fixed moment. Independent evaluators rarely obtain the access to model internals that these methods assume. The methods also speak to what a model does, while the harder question is how far a company's public claims have run ahead of the evidence.

  • Tort liability presupposes legal infrastructure most deployment settings lack.

    A study of a well-resourced legal system found that fewer than 2% of patients harmed by negligent adverse events ever filed a malpractice claim. Where liability infrastructure is thin and suing a health AI vendor has never been tested in court, tort recovery is effectively unavailable. A missed diagnosis from a miscalibrated algorithm leaves no trace.

None of the three follows a system continuously, in the place it is actually used, on terms the affected community controls. That is the gap SAKSHI is built for.

The platform

Three elements

  • Trained local auditors

    Community members in the settings where health AI is deployed are trained and certified to run the investigation themselves. They apply the protocol, reach the findings, and hold the record. See Governance.

  • A standardized four-domain protocol

    Every profile examines clinical evidence, the regulatory pathway, financial disclosures, and patient experience, using the same questions at every hub so findings can be compared across contexts. See The Protocol.

  • A public evidence record

    Findings are published as a profile that is updated as the deployed system changes, with the company's response and every correction on the record. See Profiles.

Where it begins

Low- and middle-income countries first

The accountability gap is widest in low- and middle-income countries. A scoping review of twelve African countries found that none had an authority mandated to regulate AI in healthcare, and the communities living with the results of deployment often have no way to know that an AI system is part of their care at all. SAKSHI's first profiles are planned for these settings.

The same conditions, clinical claims that cannot be checked and communities with no way to challenge what enters their health systems, also turn up inside high-income regulatory sandboxes. The methodology is meant to extend to those settings as the profile database grows.

Where things stand

A proposal under development

SAKSHI is a design and a governance model, not yet an operating platform. No profiles have been published. The framework's claims about comparability and feasibility have not been tested against real profiles produced by independently constituted teams. The plan for testing them is set out on the About page.

The first regional hubs are being formed now. If you are a medical student, clinician, patient advocate, data scientist, or public health practitioner in a setting where health AI is deployed, read how to get involved or write to lcp-sakshi@mit.edu.
